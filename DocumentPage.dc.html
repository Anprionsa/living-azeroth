A Living Azeroth

Systems design for a world that reads as inhabited rather than as a static diorama with mobs standing in assigned boxes.

The chronicles of this world are full of wars its roads have never carried. The camps face each other across the grass, and nothing happens.

The quest text says the Kolkar are breaking through Horde lines. The zone descriptions say the Venture Company is stripping Stonetalon bare. The faction ledgers say the Steamwheedle Cartel and the Venture Company are at open commercial war. A traveler on those roads would see none of it.

Almost every proposal here follows the same method: find something the existing lore already asserts, and make the world show it.

As of August 2026, Blizzard has not announced a product called "Classic+". What is on the record is narrower: at the State of Azeroth stream on January 29, 2026, Blizzard said that clarity on Classic's future and Anniversary realm progression would come at BlizzCon 2026, confirmed for September 12 and 13 in Anaheim.

Everything else is community inference built on a real but ambiguous evidence base: the "Project Camelot" datamine, a sustained run of encrypted internal builds tagged around version 1.60, private server takedowns, and a May 2026 creator summit under NDA.

This document assumes nothing about what Blizzard is actually building. It is a design proposal, not a prediction.

Every proposal in this document is sorted on five independent axes. Read linearly, the disagreement between them is invisible. Pivoted, it is the argument: cheap behavioral work is what makes the expensive architectural work viable, and risk, cost, and deployment path are three different questions.

The cheapest immersion win in the game is idle animation variety tied to what a faction does for a living. None of it asks for new combat behavior or quest hooks. The whole brief is work, rest, and the hours between shifts.

The graveyards of Duskwood are worked at night, and not by mourners. The robbers the zone already posts there stand still at their plots; give them the work the fiction assigns them. Kneel and dig, pry at a lid, straighten up, move to the next grave. A rare variant has two of them shoving each other over something pulled from the earth, which sells criminals rather than sentries. Duskwood already carries a grave robbing story through Abercrombie, and the ambient behavior should look like the same world that produced him.

Windshear Crag has been stripped to the stumps by Venture Company logging under Gerenzo Wrenchwhistle, and the zone shows almost none of the work in motion. Let the chain be seen end to end: a chopper crew felling a marked tree, a shredder hauling the log to the stacking yard, a foreman checking loads between hauls. The stacked lumber is the cargo for Section 2.

The same argument runs through Bloodsail camps that are patrol grids instead of camps, Dark Iron crews probing Loch Modan toward Ironforge, and the Booty Bay docks, which are a functioning port in fiction and a static backdrop in practice.

Section 1.6

Routines rather than headcount

Adding NPCs without adding behavior produces a crowd of statues. Stormwind's orphans play near the Cathedral steps by day and are called inside at dusk. Ironforge miners travel the road out toward Gol'Bolar Quarry instead of standing at a fixed post. Grunts spar in the Ring of Valor while patrols rotate between the Valley of Strength and the Valley of Wisdom. Booty Bay is already the chaos city by design and should be the densest and most visibly transactional of the four.

Section 1.8

The Park district and the guild that stopped working

Stormwind's Park district is unfinished and the reason is standing in the keep. The Stonemasons Guild rebuilt the city after the Second War, was not paid, and was ordered disbanded when Edwin VanCleef demanded restitution. None of that needs narrating. It needs dressing: idle masons, unfinished stonework, tools and cut blocks left where a crew walked off. A player who has run the Deadmines and then notices the Park has assembled the connection themselves.

Veron Amberstill breeds rams outside Kharanos. The job is in the quest text and the title over his head, and nowhere in what he actually does with his hands.

Reference · Dun Morogh

The roads of this world were paved for traffic they have never carried. Put carts and wagons on them in earnest, on real routes, which a player can ride beside, escort, rob, or simply watch go past. This is the load-bearing feature of the whole document, because it connects the ambient layer to the economic layer.

Vanilla already contains every component. It has escort quests with NPCs pathing long distances under attack, including Miran's run through Loch Modan and Gilthares Firebough's run from Northwatch Hold to Ratchet. It has NPCs running circuits between named locations, notably the Defias Messenger. What it does not have is that behavior as standing world furniture rather than as a quest object.

Kalimdor's web should read as opportunistic and recent: Venture lumber down the Talondeep Path, a supply wagon on the Gold Road subject to Kolkar raids, a second Venture route through the Shimmering Flats toward Gadgetzan. Eastern Kingdoms should read as centuries deep. The accounts hold Loch Modan among the most peaceful regions on the continent behind Algaz Gate, and its routes should feel worn in: standing checkpoints, established schedules, roads with names.

Section 2.6

The cost of robbery

The answer is already sitting in vanilla's reputation system. Steamwheedle reputation is a single shared bar across four cities, so dropping far enough makes bruisers hostile in all of them at once and costs auction house access at three. Venture wagons are already hostile, so they cost nothing and are the entry-level target. Steamwheedle wagons are the only version with an actual decision inside them. Repeatedly hitting cartel cargo should raise Bloodsail standing, which turns that grind from killing bruisers until they hate you into actually behaving like a blockade runner.

Section 2.8

Route funneling and the chokepoints the map already has

A caravan system with traffic spread evenly across the map is scenery. One where a few crossings carry nearly everything has geography with stakes, and the chokepoints already exist. Every crossing between the continents runs through a boat. The land route from Arathi crosses the Thandol Span. Dun Algaz is the only pass from the Wetlands toward Loch Modan. Weight the routes accordingly and Menethil becomes the most valuable point on the continent.

Nothing about destroying a wagon should reward camping one. The cargo is the reward, it is finite per wagon, and the destruction carries no kill credit, no honor, and no bonus. A level sixty parked on the Gold Road killing every caravan that passes should be wasting an afternoon for nothing.

The Steamwheedle Cartel controls Booty Bay, Ratchet, Gadgetzan, and Everlook. It is neutral by policy, profitable because of that neutrality, and in direct commercial competition with the Venture Company, with neither side above sabotage or murder. A fully formed commercial war sits unused in the fiction: Venture appears only as hostile mobs at work sites, Steamwheedle only as auction house stops, and nothing on any road connects them.

Venture is the extraction arm and Steamwheedle is the buyer and redistributor. The friction point is a shipment moving between Ratchet and Booty Bay, harassed by Bloodsail raiders along the Stranglethorn coast, which turns the Bloodsail from a static reputation grind into a visible commercial threat.

The strongest argument for the whole network is not mechanical. It is that the Steamwheedle cities physically cannot supply themselves, and the map has been saying so since 2004.

All four sit where nobody else wanted to be. Booty Bay is built into a cliff face in hostile jungle. Ratchet is a landing on a barren coast. Gadgetzan is in the middle of a desert. Everlook is in a frozen valley above a cloud bank, reachable on foot only through a furbolg tunnel. Everlook cannot produce what Everlook sells. So the caravan system is not something being added to the world. It is something the world has implied for twenty years and never drawn.

Vanilla's beasts are combat units with animal models. Every one of them notices you at the same radius, approaches the same way, and stands in the same place until it does.

A wolf pack worth the name sends one or two ahead, low and at a distance, before the rest commit together. Bears keep to themselves and range widely between a den and a feeding ground. Crocolisks sit submerged and breach at the bank rather than standing visible in shallow water. Spiders anchor to a web rather than roaming. Turtles retreat or flee instead of fighting, because passive creatures should have a passive identity rather than being aggressive creatures with lower numbers.

Rest cycles carry the largest gameplay consequence in the section. A pack that beds down near a den for a real window, aggro-immune at range and waking only if physically disturbed, creates a live decision: sneak past, or wake the den deliberately because you want the kills.

Section 4.6

Creatures reacting to the dead

Death in Azeroth is inert. A corpse greys out and despawns and nothing registers that it was ever there. Make corpses attractants, and the delay is the whole thing: scavengers must never spawn onto a fresh kill. Thirty seconds to a couple of minutes before anything arrives. Instant scavenging reads as a mechanic aimed at the player. A delay reads as the world noticing a body on its own schedule. The common case is that the player has already moved on and never sees it, and that is correct.

Feeding differs by scavenger, because the animation is the storytelling. Ghouls crouch and feed in place. Carrion birds land and pick. Spiders wrap the corpse and haul it back toward the web, which is the only scavenger that removes the body rather than sitting on it. Oozes flow over and dissolve.

A stretch of road where players keep dying silently accrues attention, and past a threshold the place starts to feel wrong: more carrion birds circling than the zone should carry, a ghoul that wandered in from further out than its normal range. Attention decays, which is what keeps it from becoming a farm. A veteran rides down a road, notices the birds are heavier than they should be, and understands that people have been dying here. That is the world telling the player something true about itself, expressed as circling crows.

Desolace is the one place this should not accumulate. Kodo canonically go there to die, so scavenger presence is permanent and heavy from the start. An arrested zone with one functioning system inside it is more unsettling than an empty one.

There is a behavioral gap between beasts and civilized factions that vanilla never dramatizes. Gnolls, murlocs, kobolds, harpies, and quilboar have camps, hierarchy, tools, and territory, but they are not the Defias or the Scarlet Crusade. They should read as neither.

Gnolls are built on hyena social structure, so show a crude hierarchy: a larger named model eating first, smaller ones squabbling over what is left, and stolen goods piled in a camp rather than existing only in a drop table. Murlocs are the best case in the game for just above animal, because their identity is a screeching alarm network rather than individuals. Kobolds should be visibly the weakest of the tier, prone to breaking as a group when their strongest member dies. Harpies perch and dive rather than holding ground camps.

Quilboar have the strongest lore in the tier and it points somewhere specific. Agamaggan was killed during the War of the Ancients, and where his blood fell, thorn-ridden vines rose from the ground.

It is his blood and not his quills, which changes the image entirely. The Razorfen is not a carcass the quilboar live inside. It is what the soil did afterward. That reframes the race: quilboar do not build, they tend. A camp should read as horticulture rather than construction, with thorn walls grown and trained rather than assembled and no cut ends anywhere in the structure.

It also hands Section 4 something it otherwise lacks, a plant that behaves like a creature. Thorns with a slow growth response, thickening where quilboar tend them and receding where they have been driven off, are the clearest possible readout of who currently holds a piece of ground. Quilboar defend ground. Gnolls raid it. That distinction should be legible without reading a single quest.

The thing that ruins an event is a visible timer. If players can schedule around it, it stops being the world's business and becomes content queued up for them. So flare-ups are driven by conditions the player cannot see, accumulating quietly, with no counter, no bar, and no announcement. Most of the time nothing happens, and when it does there is a good chance you are not close enough to participate.

Westfall is the strongest candidate, because the Defias and the Riverpaw are already there and already hostile. Track recent Defias deaths as a proxy for the gang looking weak, and past a threshold a one-time raid spawns with both sides fighting each other rather than the player. If you are nearby, you see it. If you are not, you arrive later to find gnoll and Defias corpses tangled together and have to work out what happened. The second outcome is arguably the better one, and it will be the common one.

Desolace deserves special attention, because vanilla already built the mechanic and then hid it in a reputation grind. The centaur clans are mortal enemies by design and each kill moves the opposing bar. The feud is fully specified and completely invisible in the world.

Section 6.7

Flare-ups and quest integrity

A flare-up that eats a quest objective is a bug wearing a feature's clothes. Named NPCs are never casualties. Casualties come from generic spawns only, capped as a fraction of the camp. Respawn accelerates rather than pauses, because the player who arrives afterward did not opt into the event. And a flare-up cannot fire on a camp with players in combat inside it, which also means the player generating the hidden condition has to leave before the event pays off.

Section 6.9

Choosing between three kinds of world state

Cyclical state is the default at every level. It answers the hardest objection against a living world, which is that a game with a continuous supply of new players cannot ratchet. Anything permanent is a door that closes behind the people who were there. A cycle closes no doors, and it produces knowledge no static world can: a veteran who says the Defias are worse than they were is describing something true and checkable.

One-way additive events are permitted when nothing is removed. The gates of Ahn'Qiraj are the proof. The useful lesson is not that permanent change is for max level, it is that permanent change must be additive. Finishing the Lakeshire bridge would be safe at any level, because a finished bridge takes nothing away.

Phasing is almost never correct. It gives each player a private copy of world state, and it is the specific technology that made the modern game's world feel lonely. A flare-up matters because the person you tell about it can go and see it.

Additive changes may be permanent, subtractive changes must be cyclical. Never phase anything two players might reasonably need to stand in together. No hubs, no roads, no zones.

A timber wolf in Ashenvale drops silver coins. Nothing in the fiction explains this, and it breaks immersion at a rate of several times per minute during leveling.

Beasts drop no coin, ever. Bandit humanoids are the primary gold source, because the Defias, the Syndicate, the Bloodsail, and the Southsea pirates are written as organized criminal enterprises and should be where money physically is. Cultists drop reagents and minimal coin, because they are fanatics rather than looters. Zones carry economic character too: Westfall is poor across the board, Un'goro has no coin at any level, and Stranglethorn is conspicuously plentiful, which is what makes the other two read as intentional rather than stingy.

Swallowed remains are the exception that makes the rule work. A very low chance for a predator to yield something it ate rather than something it owned: a gnawed silver coin from a Duskwood wolf, scorched research notes from a devilsaur, Bloodhoof-marked jewelry from a Thousand Needles predator. Everything comes up bent, tooth-marked, or partly digested, and the flavor text does the storytelling with no quest attached.

Section 7.7

Anatomy that reads from the outside

Vanilla's materials divide into legible and illegible. Herbs, ore, leather, and cloth are all visible from outside the creature or the node. Venom sacs are not, and neither are flame sacs, ichor, glands, or hearts. The item is the only evidence that the anatomy exists at all.

The strongest fix costs almost nothing, because the information is already in the combat log. If a creature envenoms you, it has a venom sac. Tighten that correlation until it holds without exception and every poison debuff a player has ever taken becomes a drop-table lesson they learned without being taught.

The instinct with an undifferentiated reagent is to split it into spider, scorpid, and serpent variants. Do not. Bag space is the real currency of the vanilla leveling experience, and fragmenting one reagent into five spends the player's scarcest resource to buy texture.

Add texture to materials without adding item slots. Naming, flavor, visual tells, behavioral tells, and source logic are all free. New item IDs are expensive.

A Defias highwayman carries the tools of the job he is doing. Loot logic asks only that what drops is what he was seen holding.

Systems imported from The Burning Crusade forward get rebuilt around vanilla's mechanics rather than transplanted. Gem sockets do not exist in Classic, so jewelcrafting has to be a finished-goods profession: rings, necklaces, and trinkets crafted whole, competing against dungeon and quest reward jewelry rather than augmenting existing gear. Inscription produces scrolls and offhand codices instead of glyphs, which keeps it away from the talent system entirely.

Woodcutting gives lumber an economic identity instead of leaving it as Venture Company backstory, supplies the cart network with a crafted input, and creates direct competitive friction with the Windshear Crag operation, since players and goblins would be stripping the same forests. Trapping is the only proposal in this document that interacts with the wildlife systems without killing anything.

Section 8.7 and 8.9

Keeping low-level goods valuable

Peacebloom becomes economically worthless the moment a player outlevels Elwynn, even though it keeps growing there forever. Blended recipes cut expensive components with cheap bases the way real formulation does. Dual crafting paths offer the same output fast and expensive or slow and cheap. A level sixty alchemist going back to farm silverleaf because it is the cost-effective route, not because a quest sent them, is the goal state.

Separately, coarse thread, weak flux, and empty vials are bought from vendors and produced by no player. Every one of those transactions is a dead gold sink that bypasses the player economy. Vanilla already treats crystal vials as a tradeable commodity of some standing. The game knows these are goods. It just does not let players make them.

Section 8.10 and 8.11

Wandering trainers and the problem with hidden content

Every leatherworking specialization master already sits in a wilderness camp. The pattern is in the game. It is just nailed to the ground. A wandering trainer is a traveler first and a trainer second: she makes camp, eats, packs, leaves. A player who happens to be in Thelsamar the week she is there gets something. A player who was there last week finds out from a guard that he missed her.

The honest problem is that a trainer on a fixed route gets datamined inside a week. So the route is not fixed: she picks each next stop from a small weighted set, and the community can learn her territory without being able to predict tomorrow. The goal was never that players cannot find her. It is that finding her is a thing you do in the world, using the world, rather than in a browser tab.

Selling to an NPC vendor stocks that vendor's inventory rather than deleting the item and conjuring gold from nowhere. Shelves reflect what has actually been sold locally, and they can run dry. If nobody has been supplying flux in Ironforge, Ironforge runs low on flux, and a player who notices can import from somewhere with surplus. That is emergent commerce created by nothing more than a stock counter.

The obvious failure mode is a guild flooding one vendor to crash local prices, or starving one to manufacture a shortage. The mitigation is a soft cap: a vendor pays well for the first N of an item per day, then its buy price drops sharply. That makes mass dumping unprofitable without penalizing a normal player offloading crafting byproducts, and it means manipulation requires sustained coordination rather than one afternoon.

A wagon that completes its route credits stock to the destination's vendor pool. A wagon that does not arrive does not.

That single sentence is the payoff for building the cart network at all. An intercepted Ironforge shipment means Ironforge is short on flux the following week. Nothing announces it. It is simply true, and it is noticeable to whoever went to buy flux. This is the first mechanic in the document where one player's action changes what a different player finds on a shelf, without either of them knowing about the other.

Section 9.5

The interdiction the lore already ran

Westfall's poverty is canonically a supply interdiction plot. Onyxia, from the shadows as Lady Prestor, cut off supplies and support to keep the Defias enraged. What the player sees is wagons that should reach Sentinel Hill and do not, and a vendor whose stock stays thinner than any comparable settlement's for reasons observable on the road rather than stated in quest text. A zone that is being starved on purpose is more interesting than a zone that is simply poor.

Azeroth's zones have fixed weather. Elwynn is always late spring, Winterspring is always deep winter, Tanaris is always noon in a desert. The world has climate but no calendar, which means it has no sense of time passing at all.

The model is one rule applied everywhere rather than a pile of per-zone exceptions: latitude for temperature plus a per-zone moisture value. The correction found while working Kalimdor zone by zone is that latitude alone predicts the bottom of both continents is productive, which is wrong. Tanaris and Silithus sit at the same latitude as Un'goro and they are desert. Moisture is what decides whether a warm zone is jungle or sand. Two dials instead of one, and still buildable.

The full classification of both continents into full turn, muted pulse, and arrest is set out in View 6 below.

The muted pulse is lighting, weather tables, and audio with no gameplay hooks attached, so it ships with the art work in Layer 1. Arrest costs nothing at all, since it means doing nothing. Only the full turn is Tier 3.

Winterspring is already permanently in one season, which is why it is the control case rather than a candidate. Snow here means nothing changed.

These proposals are not equally expensive, and more importantly they are not equally reversible. A live-service game can patch in animation work indefinitely. It cannot casually retrofit a new economic model onto a server that has been running for eight months on the old one.

The fresh-realm-as-testbed model resolves the Tier 3 problem cleanly, and Blizzard has already run the play. Launch Tier 3 changes only on a new realm, run Tier 1 and Tier 2 as ongoing content, and fold forward what works. The important constraint is that folding forward should require player appetite on the originating realm, not just internal enthusiasm. A system that works mechanically but that its own playerbase does not want is not a success.

The tiers are written as pre-launch advice, and that framing has a problem: if Classic+ is already built and running, Tier 3 stops meaning risky and starts meaning too late. So the rest of Section 11 assumes the game shipped and asks a different question. Not how risky is this, but what can be added to a live world, in what order, and what does each addition require to already be there.

Section 11.4

Additive versus revisionary

The distinction that matters in a live game is not cost. It is whether a change invalidates something players have already learned. Additive changes put something in the world that was not there, and a player who knew the old world is not wrong about anything. Revisionary changes alter something already known and are expensive in player trust rather than in engineering.

This cuts across the tiers rather than aligning with them. The behavioral tell audit is Tier 2 by engineering risk and nearly harmless in isolation, but it relocates drop sources players have had memorized for twenty years. Conversely, material camps are new locations that take nothing away, so despite touching the world map they are as safe as an animation pass.

On live realms, ship additive freely and treat revisionary as a fresh-realm item regardless of tier.
Section 11.6 and 11.8

Hooks and the proposals that split

The thing that cannot be retrofitted is not a system, it is a hook. A per-zone climate state, a route and cargo entity, vendor stock stored as a variable rather than a constant. Any of these can ship inert, doing nothing visible, costing almost nothing. Ship the hooks even when the systems are years out, and ship them switched off. That is the cheapest decision in this entire document and the only one that expires.

Several proposals appear in more than one deployment bucket, and that is not indecision. The visible half can ship to live now, the consequential half needs a fresh realm. Carts as traffic are scenery; carts as the thing that determines vendor stock are architectural. By the time the consequential version launches, players have spent months seeing wagons on the roads, so the system arrives already legible instead of needing to teach itself.

The three deployment paths are laid out in View 1, and the dependency ordering in View 2.

Every other section proposes a system. This one proposes a stance, because the systems will be built by someone with an art budget and the way that budget gets spent will decide whether any of it looks like Classic.

Retail's problem was never polygon count. It is that higher fidelity pushes everything toward a single house style, because detailed assets are expensive and expensive assets get reused. Classic reads as characterful partly because the art had to carry meaning through silhouette and color rather than surface detail, and silhouette is what actually reads at distance across a zone.

Spend the art budget on variety, not fidelity. Same texture resolution, same polygon budget, more distinct assets.

Southshore and Darkshire are both human and should feel almost nothing alike. What separates real buildings inside a single culture is four practical constraints, and vanilla has all four available and unused: materials, meaning whatever is within hauling distance; climate, which mostly means roof pitch; threat, which is the most legible of the four; and age. A player should be able to guess how dangerous a region is from the buildings before meeting anything in it.

Section 12.3

Where the material comes from

The game already did this once, completely. Durotar has no forests, Orgrimmar is built from timber, the Warsong log Ashenvale, and the route is already drawn from the Lumber Camp through Mor'shan Base Camp and south. Warsong Gulch is a battleground fought over construction material. Vanilla built one complete material chain, made a battleground out of it, and then never applied the idea to a single other settlement.

Sentinel Hill needs timber and Westfall has almost none. Putting a logging camp near the Duskwood bridge is correct; adding trees to the southern hills is a trap. Westfall's treelessness is the zone's entire character, and adding a local supply solves a problem the zone exists to have. The external camp makes the dependency visible and gives interdiction something concrete to cut. Timber crossing that bridge is also timber from a cursed forest, which is a better explanation of Sentinel Hill's character than any amount of art direction.

By risk this is Tier 1. It is pure art, it ships whenever, it breaks nothing, and it can be patched in one settlement at a time. By cost it is closer to Tier 3, because it is a large number of art hours with no way to shortcut them. Any team reading Section 11 as a rough cost ordering would badly underestimate this section.

The world grows and gets smaller. Expansion zones ship in batches tied to a level bracket, are placed outside the existing world so they inherit no traffic, carry one-time content, and get invalidated by the next expansion. The active portion is always the newest slice and everything behind it is scenery you fly over.

Vanilla accidentally ran the experiment. Un'goro stays busy because of herbs and crystals. Winterspring stays busy because of Everlook and Black Lotus. Meanwhile Loch Modan, Redridge, Darkshore, Stonetalon, and Thousand Needles are ghost towns above level 30. A zone survives if it has a reason that is not leveling. One is usually enough. None is fatal.

A patch can add one zone, and it can add it inward.

The vanilla map is full of gaps, closed gates, and places the world points at and never lets you reach. An inward addition inherits the existing road network, has no clean level bracket, is purely additive, and is already promised. The wall, the gate, and the locked door are all in the game. That is this document's method applied at the largest possible scale: everywhere else the argument is to show what the lore already asserts, and here the lore asserts a place and the map draws its front door.

The clearest doors are Karazhan from Deadwind Pass, where the tower is modeled and standing and was cut from vanilla for time; the Emerald Dream from the four Dream Gates placed in patch 1.8, which are guarded and lead nowhere; Gilneas behind the Greymane Wall; Grim Batol from the Wetlands; Quel'Thalas past the blocked Thalassian Pass; and Azshara, which is not a closed door but an unfinished room.

By reversibility a new zone is Tier 1, the most purely additive thing possible. By cost it is Tier 3 and then some. New zones are safe to add and expensive to add, which is exactly the profile that suits a long slow content cadence and exactly the profile that suits nothing else.

A world buff is a reward for having been somewhere. The head of an enemy of the realm is carried to a city gate and cried from the steps, and for two hours everyone within earshot fights a little harder. A songflower blooms in Felwood and whoever finds it is the better for the rest of the afternoon. These are the strongest argument the game makes for leaving the capital, and the raiding of them turned that argument inside out.

What went wrong was never the buffs. It was what a raid roster did with them. A guild that expected them required them, so the hours before a raid night went on standing in Orgrimmar waiting for a horn, and a player who lost one to a disconnect had lost the evening rather than a bonus. The reward for going out into the world became a toll paid before being allowed to play, which is the opposite of what it was for.

Here they are suppressed on entering a raid instance or an instanced battleground, and restored on leaving. They are not lost.

Dungeons are untouched, and so is the world itself, so a buff still pays everywhere it was earned. Season of Mastery suppressed world buffs in raids and did not give them back; Classic Era banks them through an item the player has to remember to carry and use. Restoring them on the way out asks nothing of anyone and removes the part that made them resented, which was the prerequisite and never the buff. Boss health is tuned as though the raid were unbuffed, because it is.

Suppressed on entering a raid instance or an instanced battleground and restored on leaving. Dungeons, world content and open-world combat are unaffected. The ruleset this project assumes is recorded in the talent dataset as classic-plus, which takes Season of Mastery as its basis and carries this as its one refinement.

Everything above this point is behavior laid over the world as it already exists. This view is the other half of the argument: the map has room, and most of the room is land vanilla shipped as a border rather than as a place. Each of these is a community map of a proposed zone, drawn against the real terrain and marked with the same vocabulary the client uses for quest hubs, dungeons, and raids.

They are included as scope, not as specification. A living world that gains nine zones needs the systems in sections 1 through 10 more than a static one does, because nine empty zones is nine times the emptiness. The dependency runs in that direction and not the other.

Second document

Behavior over numbers

What a talent point should buy, and 27 vanilla trees rebuilt to the answer. Version 1.0, August 2026.

The document above describes a world. The document below argues that post-vanilla classes should arrive as trees rather than as classes. This one sits upstream of both. Its claim is that a talent point should buy behavior and never a number, and its evidence is all 27 vanilla trees rebuilt to that rule, in order of severity. What follows on this page is the five constraints the rebuild accepted, two audit views that measure what a vanilla point buys and show one tree before and after, the eight proposals that carry the rule into the trees, and the 27 rebuilds with their diagnoses and floors. The class document inherits its rules and the simulation document tests them. The calculator at the bottom of the page holds every tree in full.

The tree structure stays: three trees, seven tiers, five points per tier to advance, 51 points at level 60, a capstone at tier 7. A player who last logged out in 2006 should open the panel and recognise it. What changes is what the nodes contain.

Structure

The tree shape is not the problem and does not change. Seven rows, five points to advance, one capstone.

Recognition

This is not a departure. The panel a returning player opens is the panel they remember.

What a point buys

Behavior, never numbers. No talent should exist whose entire text is a percentage.

Where numbers go

Flat stats stay in the game and stop being purchased. They are granted for depth in a tree rather than bought with a point.

Power

Net neutral against a canonical vanilla build. A redistribution of what points do, not a buff.

The complaint is usually stated as a feeling. The number is worse than the feeling. Every node in all 27 vanilla trees is tagged by what it buys: a number, an effect, or a button. The dataset is parsed from a 1.12 talent calculator, and the classifier agrees with an independent hand audit of Arms exactly.

A harder measure than counting flat points is the forced-flat floor: take every behavior talent the moment it becomes available, fill with flat only when nothing else is legal, and count. That number is the minimum filler the tree forces on the way to its capstone. No build, no preference, no argument. The floor for the selected tree is shown below the grid, and it is a lower bound rather than a prediction: rogue proves a healthy tree can still produce flat builds when the flat talents are simply stronger.

 Why it is here

Four eras of talent design and three other games sit behind these. Vanilla had progression without meaningful choice, Mists of Pandaria had choice without progression, and Dragonflight got both and paid for it with permanence. Diablo 4's Lord of Hatred, shipped April 2026, is the closest case study: it stripped every dedicated passive node out of the class trees and moved raw scaling to items and Paragon, on the stated reasoning that legacy passives diluted combat engagement.

Separation of concerns is the whole trick. Gear carries the numbers, tree depth carries the passive curve, talent points carry behavior. Vanilla has all three doing all three jobs, which is why none of them feels decisive.

5.1

The depth dividend

Every point spent in a tree grants an automatic passive appropriate to that tree. Fifteen points in Fury gives you the Fury curve, and which fifteen is irrelevant. Every flat bonus currently sitting in the trees goes here. The coefficient is set by summing what a canonical build already buys, so the character ends up with the same numbers and roughly sixteen points come free.

5.2

Ranks stack effects

Multi-rank nodes stay. A rank adds a discrete effect rather than increasing a number. Improved Rend becomes Bloodletting: rank 1 ignores armor, rank 2 lets Overpower refresh it, rank 3 dumps the remaining damage when the target falls below 20%. Same node, same three ranks, nothing scales. This is roughly 150 nodes across nine classes and it is the largest line item in the document.

5.3

Tier 5 is the hybrid seat

A tier gate is points already invested, so a tier-N talent costs 5(N-1) plus 1. Tier 7 is 31, tier 6 is 26, tier 5 is 21. An earlier draft read 51 minus 31 as 20 and called that the tier 5 seat; 20 points reaches tier 4. The budget offers two honest shapes instead: 31/20 for a capstone and a shallow second tree, or 30/21 and 26/25 for no capstone and two deep talents. Warlock is the only vanilla class with two competing viable raid builds, and both take the second shape.

5.4

Subtraction talents

Two per tree at most, optional rather than capstone, because a forced subtraction reads as a nerf while an optional one reads as a choice. Sure Strike removes your ability to critically strike and in exchange your attacks can never be avoided. Shadowform is the vanilla precedent, which means the design space is already established in the era.

5.5

World-facing depth rewards

Talents that do not help you fight are never taken, so world-facing effects are not talents. They ride on the depth dividend at 15 and 30 points. A beast mastery hunter reads where a zone's beasts moved with the season. A subtlety rogue can pickpocket caravan drivers. Each depends on a world system from the first document, and the list is cut entirely if those systems do not ship.

5.6

Unconventional builds and the dividend curve

The dividend curve is linear, so spreading points costs no stats: a 26/25 build and a 31/20 build receive the same dividend total, and the difference lives entirely in which talents they own. Depth is rewarded by discrete talents, never by an accelerating curve. The checkable rule: two tier-5 seats plus a tier-2 should land in the same band as a capstone plus a tier-4. The simulation document holds this to sixty of sixty cells.

5.7

Revealing interdependencies

Cross-tree interdependencies are found rather than read, and the game tells you about them somewhere other than a tooltip. Nothing stays secret past launch week, so the fiction of learning is the reward, not the information. The binding rule: a cross-tree conditional may never be the reason to take the talent it sits on. The talent is worth its points alone; the conditional is upside. Ignorance costs upside, never correctness.

5.8

One set of talents for both modes

No PvP-only nodes, no PvP branch, no assumption that future gear bridges the gap. Prefer graded effects to binary ones: a slow or an armor reduction works on a boss at reduced relevance, a stun works at zero. Where a binary effect is the right fantasy it keeps a graded fallback that lands on an immune target. Three checks for any rewritten talent, and most trees needed one change or none.

The depth dividend does not calibrate against an absorbed tree, because there is no canonical vanilla build to sum. So absorbed trees grant no stat curve at all. That is the better answer anyway: a free curve on a fourth tree is exactly the mechanism by which that tree becomes a tax, and a protection paladin who gains armor for fifteen points in Blackguard will take fifteen points in Blackguard.

Third document

Class absorption

New archetypes as trees, not classes. Version 1.2, August 2026.

One document describes the world. This one describes something you go into that world to get. Every tree here is acquired through a chain running through named vanilla locations, which is why they share a page.

There are ten trees, of two kinds, and the distinction matters enough that the document gives it its own section. Seven are absorbed: a post-vanilla class collapsed into a tree its host class acquires. Three are original, built for classes that absorb nothing: Chronomancer for mage, Dreamer for druid, Radiance for priest. All ten are in the expanded configuration. The original trees carry the heavier burden of proof, since no shipped version stands behind them, and the simulation below holds Dreamer and Radiance to it: both are in band, Radiance on its healing score with the combined score reported beside it.

Metamorphosis is adapted from the Warcraft III Demon Hunter ability and is visually based on Illidan's demon form. Blizzard implemented it as a Demonology warlock talent in Wrath of the Lich King, at the bottom of the tree, where it sat for three expansions as the defining ability of that spec. It was removed from warlocks in patch 7.0.3, specifically because the Demon Hunter class was being introduced.

So the direction of travel is the reverse of what people assume. The demon hunter fantasy lived inside an existing class first, worked there for years, and was taken out to make room for a class. This proposal is not inventing anything. It is declining to make that trade.

Three more precedents sit behind it. Druid shipped in 2004 as one class containing three roles, three armor-relevant forms, and three separate resources, which demonstrates the exact technology this depends on. Season of Discovery gave vanilla classes roles they never had through runes, and delivered warlock tanking through a chain running Redridge, Darkshore, the Barrens, Shadowfang Keep, Blackfathom Deeps, and a summoning ritual at Demon Fall Ridge. And The War Within introduced hero talents: subtrees layered onto existing classes, shared between two specs rather than owned by one, which is the fluid model arrived at independently twenty years later.

Do not add classes. Add trees. A class needs three specs whether or not it has three ideas, a starting experience, an itemization lane, and faction and race decisions. A class cannot be shipped in a patch. A tree can.

Each tree holds seven tiers. The first is open from the first point, and every tier after it asks for five more points spent in the tree, so the twenty-point mark costs twenty-one points to reach and the capstone costs thirty-one. Each tree carries two talents worth building toward rather than one. The mark at twenty is the hybrid’s prize and works without deep investment; the capstone is a thing a character either has or does not.

No absorbed tree brings a new resource bar with it. Each one layers a mechanic of its own onto whatever the host class already spends, the way combo points and soul shards already do.

Measured against the vanilla trees they sit beside, not one of the ten spends a point on a flat number, where 73% of vanilla’s points do. Every one of them puts at least eight points at the twenty-point mark, gives two thirds of its points or more to the mechanic it is named for, and closes at least one door for good.

Named for something it does

Between 66% and 79% of every tree’s points engage the mechanic it is named for. Conduit gives two thirds of its points to empowering, Metamorphosis more than three quarters to fel corruption, and no tree here is named for something it barely carries.

The twenty-point mark

At least eight points sit at gate 20 in all ten trees, enough that a character who stops there has bought something real. It is what a hybrid reaches for without committing to a capstone, and the reason a second tree is worth twenty-one points rather than nothing.

Every tree closes a door

Each of the ten carries at least one subtraction node, a talent whose cost is written as a real effect rather than as flavour. Damnation turns the healing a blackguard would receive into damage and stops them being healed at all. Sole Survivor arms a trapper’s traps instantly and takes the pet away.

The seven absorbed trees were drafted before these rules existed and were audited against them afterwards. More than half their points bought flat numbers, gate 20 carried six points instead of eight, Metamorphosis held almost none of the mechanic it was named for, and not one talent in any of them read the rest of the character. All four are answered in the data below, the last of them by a cross-tree talent at the twenty-point mark in each tree.

Fourth document

The simulation

Whether the tuning rule holds, measured rather than asserted. August 2026.

The talent document sets a checkable rule and the class document inherits it: a capstone build and a mid-tree build should land in the same band. This document is the check. It runs three instruments across five fight scenarios at 120 seeds per cell, and 124 of 125 banded cells sit inside -7% to +7%. The one outside is Metamorphosis on switching, at about +12%, and the gap belongs to the build it is measured against rather than to the tree. Affliction loses 27% of its output when the target dies every thirty seconds and Metamorphosis loses 14%, because a build that carries its damage in periodics is punished by a target that dies before they finish. It is named rather than moved, since closing that one cell would push out the four where the tree already sits at or under the line. Necromancy, four cells out in the file two before this one, is inside on all five since it was read as the pet tree its text describes. Radiance is banded on its healing score at the design's emission and inside on all three of its cells; its combined score, its healing with the damage it emits at weight one, is measured and reported beside it and not banded.

The figures are relative, not absolute. The gear blocks are invented, and a real balance check needs Warcraft Logs data as the control. What the numbers can say is whether the design's own rule holds against the design's own trees. It holds for every core pair and on thirty-four of the absorbed trees' thirty-five cells, and it did not hold on first measurement: the Warrior pairs read +22.7% and +33.0% until the cause was traced to the gear calibration's rage economy rather than to a talent. Findings of that kind are what the check is for.

A single-target damage simulator returned no significant difference for seven of thirteen pairs, and that was three separate problems wearing the same result. Burst cooldowns vanish into a 300-second average. Ramp and uptime effects need movement or target deaths to matter. And Repentance is a stun: no damage simulator will ever measure it, and building one that appears to would be worse than admitting the gap.

So a talent's value is its profile across scenarios, not one number. Eleven talents carry an explicit not-simulable flag. Since stage I each is read by its cost, the node forced into the build its role bands against the same build without it, so gain, cost and net are three numbers on every cell; eight carry a priced cost or a priced permission, and four, Repentance, Damnation, Deep Slumber and Fixed Point, stay argued because no instrument prices what they remove.
